# Allocation fairness

Generated by `make fairness` (`eval/fairness.py`). Nothing is written to the database.

**Measure.** Share of P50 demand met = sum(min(delivered, P50)) / sum(P50), per group. Groups: the 6 counties; distance bands from the warehouse (terciles of miles: near, middle, far); pantry size (terciles of baseline households: small, medium, large). The rule: no group may be served more than 10 percentage points below the all-pantry average.

**Before** is the objective used until Stage 13: every pound toward a pantry's forecast earned the same, so the small distance penalty was the only thing separating pantries. **After** is tiered coverage: the P50 is split into 10 slices and each slice is worth 0.02 less per pound than the one before (and always more than the biggest distance difference), so a pound goes first to the pantry that has received the smallest share of its own forecast. Distance still breaks ties between equally covered pantries.

**Fairness was only visible under scarcity.** With normal stock every pantry's forecast is met and the groups differ by under 1 point, so the check passes whatever the objective. Halve the stock, which is what a short week looks like and the case a food bank exists for, and the old objective filled nearby pantries completely while the farthest band received almost nothing.

## Trade-off: what fairness costs

Pound-miles is pounds delivered times miles from the warehouse; miles per lb is the average distance a pound travels; trip miles is the one-way miles from the warehouse summed over every pantry visited on every delivery day. Spoilage is the share of perishable pounds that expired in the warehouse or were delivered beyond true demand. Short pantry-weeks is the share of pantry-weeks where any category received under 90% of true demand.

**Reading the trade-off honestly.**

- *Fairness spreads a shortage over more pantries.* A pantry counts as short when any category gets under 90% of true demand. The old objective concentrated a shortage on the far pantries (some got nothing) and kept the rest whole; the new one gives every pantry a similar partial share. So **short pantry-weeks rise even in the normal simulation** (the Wednesday-and-Friday schedule, 33.2% to 34.8%), while the worst pantry's share rises from 0% to about 55% in the half-stock case. `docs/eval/allocation-sim.md` already reported the stockout target as not met before this change; this widens that gap slightly, and the report now says so.
- *Food travels farther.* Spreading food reaches farther pantries: pound-miles rise about 20% and trip miles about 80% when stock is short, because the nearest pantries are no longer filled first. With normal stock every pantry is visited anyway and the miles are unchanged.
- *Spoilage and P50 met do not get worse.* Overall P50 met is identical and perishable loss is the same or lower.
- *A gentler version was tried and rejected.* Applying the fairness slices only to the first 30%, 50% or 70% of each forecast (a floor) gave less protection (far band at -23.2, -9.2 and -3.3 pp in the stress test) and no setting avoided the rise in short pantry-weeks (33.2%, 34.3% and 35.5%).

## What this does and does not show

- The 10-point rule is judged on groups, not single pantries; the worst pantry's own share is in the table above.
- The half-stock case is a stress test chosen to expose the behaviour, not a forecast of any week. It reuses the same synthetic demand and forecasts.
- Fairness here is equal share of forecast demand. It is not need-weighted by income or access; the food bank may want that, and it would be a policy choice for the coordinators, not the optimizer.
- The synthetic pantries have no real county or size differences in need, so group gaps here come from the optimizer alone.
